# Aninda Studio — the English standard

**Set:** 14 August 2026 · Applies to every English word the studio ships: interface
text, error messages, documentation, the guidebook, README files, the website,
proposals and invoices.

---

## The rule in one line

**Plain international English, British spelling.**

---

## Why those two things together, and why it is not a contradiction

"International English" and "British English" sound like they pull in opposite
directions. They do not, because they are answers to two different questions.

- **Vocabulary and sentence construction** — international. Written so that a
  reader whose first language is not English understands it on one pass. That is
  the part that decides whether the writing works.
- **Spelling** — British. Not out of preference: Bangladesh's own education system
  and press use British spelling, so *colour*, *organise* and *centre* are what a
  Bangladeshi reader already expects. Choosing American spelling would make the
  writing look foreign in half the audience it is written for. A spelling
  convention has to be picked; picking the one both audiences already read is the
  only sensible answer.

So: `colour`, not `color`. But never `whilst`, `amongst`, `sort out` or `quid`.

---

## The standard being followed

**ISO 24495-1:2023 — Plain language, Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines.**
It is the international standard for plain language, and its four principles are
the ones this brand writes to. Stated in the standard's own terms, readers get
what they need if the writing is:

1. **Relevant** — they find what they need.
2. **Findable** — they can locate it easily.
3. **Understandable** — they understand it on the first read.
4. **Usable** — they can act on it.

Everything below is how those four are met in practice.

## The rules

### Sentences
- One idea per sentence. Average around **15 to 20 words**; break anything over 25.
- Active voice by default. *The build failed*, not *a failure was encountered*.
- Say who does what. If a sentence has no subject, it usually should.
- Put the main point first, then the detail. A reader who stops after one sentence
  should still have the answer.

### Words
- Use the **shortest word that is still precise**. `use` not `utilise`, `about`
  not `approximately`, `start` not `commence`, `enough` not `sufficient`.
- **Precision beats simplicity when they conflict.** If a technical term is the
  correct one, use it — and explain it in one plain sentence the first time it
  appears. `contrast ratio` stays; it gets a definition.
- **No idioms, no phrasal verbs where a plain verb exists.** `cancel` not `call
  off`; `continue` not `carry on`; `find out` becomes `learn` or `check`.
  Idioms are the single largest cause of misreading for a non-native reader.
- **No metaphors from culture or sport.** No *ballpark*, *out of the box*, *touch
  base*, *home straight*, *sticky wicket*.
- **No Latin abbreviations.** Write *for example* not *e.g.*, *that is* not *i.e.*,
  *and so on* not *etc.*
- **Say the number and its unit.** *10 MB*, *220 ms*, *4.5:1*. Never *a large file*
  where a figure is known.
- One name for one thing, everywhere. If it is the *mark*, it is never also the
  *logo* or the *icon* in a different paragraph.

### Tone

The tone is **cooperative, friendly, approachable and professional**. Those are
four adjectives, and adjectives do not guide writing, so here is what each one
actually means at the keyboard.

**Cooperative — write as if sitting beside the reader, not across a desk.**
- Offer the next step, don't just state the position. *Here's what I'd suggest*,
  not *this is the procedure*.
- Use *you* and *I*, and use them in the same sentence when it's a joint problem.
  *If you send me the file, I can check it.*
- When there's a choice, give the recommendation and the reason, then leave the
  decision with them. Never present one option as though it were the only one.
- ✅ *Two ways to do this. I'd start with the first — it's quicker to undo.*
- ❌ *The recommended approach is option one.*

**Friendly — warm, not chummy.**
- Contractions where they fall naturally: *it's*, *doesn't*, *you'll*. They are
  most of what makes English sound like a person.
- Greet plainly and get on with it. No performed enthusiasm.
- Thank people for a real thing, not as a reflex. *Thanks for the file* — yes.
  *Thanks for reaching out!* — no.
- **No exclamation marks.** Warmth comes from the words, not the punctuation.
- ❌ No *Hi there!*, *Awesome!*, *Happy to help!*, *Hope this helps!*, emoji, or
  slang. Friendly is not the same as casual, and casual does not travel well
  between languages.

**Approachable — never make the reader feel they should already have known.**
- **Banned outright: *simply*, *just*, *easy*, *obviously*, *of course*, *clearly*.**
  Every one tells a reader who is stuck that the problem is them.
- Explain a technical term the first time it appears, in one sentence, without
  being asked and without making a performance of it.
- Say plainly when something is genuinely difficult. It is a relief to read.
- Invite the question: *If that doesn't match what you're seeing, tell me and I'll
  look again.*
- ✅ *Contrast ratio — how far apart two colours are in brightness. 4.5:1 is the
  minimum for body text.*
- ❌ *Simply ensure your contrast ratio is compliant.*

**Professional — the credibility that lets the other three be safe.**
- **First person singular.** It is one person, so *I*, not *we*. Using *we* to
  sound larger is the first small dishonesty a studio tells about itself.
- Be accurate before being warm. A friendly wrong answer is worse than a plain
  right one.
- Say the number and its unit. Give the date on anything that will go stale.
- **Never apologetic, never boastful.** Name a limit where it would otherwise
  surprise someone — as information, not as a confession, and never wrapped in an
  apology. One *sorry* for a real mistake; never *sorry* as a throat-clearing.
- Don't over-promise, and don't hedge everything into mush. If you know, say so.
  If you don't, say that instead.

**How the four fit together.** Cooperative and friendly decide *how it feels*;
approachable and professional decide *whether it can be trusted*. When two of them
pull against each other, the order is: **accurate first, then clear, then warm.**
A sentence is never made friendlier at the cost of being right.

### Interface text
- **Buttons are a verb with its object.** *Save the entry*, not *Submit*, not *OK*.
  A button whose label does not say what will happen is a trap.
- **Errors say what happened, then what happens next.** *Couldn't save. Your work
  is still here — try again in a moment.* Never *Error 500*. Never blame the reader.
- **Empty states say what to do.** *Nothing here yet. Add your first entry to
  begin.*
- Sentence case for everything — headings, buttons, labels. Not Title Case, not
  ALL CAPS. Capitals are harder to read and, in Bangla, meaningless.
- Never rely on colour alone. Every state carries a word and a glyph as well.

### Structure
- Headings that say what the section contains, not clever ones.
- Lists for anything with more than three parallel items.
- A table when the reader will compare things.
- Short paragraphs. Three or four sentences.

---

## What this does NOT mean

- **Not dumbed down.** Plain language is about being understood, not about having
  less to say. The technical content stays; the sentences carrying it get out of
  the way.
- **Not shorter at any cost.** A sentence that saves three words and costs the
  reader a re-read is a bad trade.
- **Not the same as the Bangla.** The Bangla is written as Bangla, not translated
  from this. Same meaning, same steps, different sentences. Where the two must
  agree exactly — a number, a licence name, a file name — they agree; everywhere
  else they are written independently, each natural in its own language.

---

## How this is checked

- Sentence length and passive-voice constructions are counted mechanically across
  every shipped English string.
- Every banned word and idiom above is in a blocklist the checker enforces.
- Anything the checker cannot judge — whether a sentence is *actually* clear — is
  named in its output as a gap rather than passed silently.

*A reader should never have to be clever to understand this studio.*
